01 · The model

One library of lessons, five curated routes through it

Every lesson is a self-contained module with its own objective, its own hands-on exercise, and its own live demo. A department picks the pathway that matches where it starts, and several pathways reuse the same core lessons, so a finance manager and a marketing executive sitting in the same room on AI Basics are getting the same foundation before their routes separate.

Three tracks

The AI track (AI-1 to AI-8) builds general capability that every department uses. The Marketing track (MK-1 to MK-4) covers marketing fundamentals for people who never trained in it. The applied track (AX-1 to AX-9) puts the two together on real work.

The ELI dial

Each lesson re-pitches its explanations across four reading levels. ELI15 is pitched for a bright teenager, ELI20 for early-career staff, ELI25 for an experienced professional outside the subject, and ELI30 for an experienced practitioner inside it. The analogies, examples, and exercises stay the same, only the depth of explanation moves. A mixed-seniority room stays in one session instead of splitting into two.

Every lesson ends with something built

Every lesson ends with a Monday action: one task the attendee already does every week, done once with AI before they leave the room. The capstone lesson, AX-8, is entirely build time, so each person on a pathway that includes it walks out with one working, documented workflow of their own. AX-8 sits in P3, P4, and P5, which covers every department recommendation in section 05.

Five lessons carry the whole program

AI-1 (AI Basics), AI-3 (Prompting and Tools), AI-5 (Agentic AI and Claude Code), AX-7 (AI CRM and Conversations), and AX-8 (Build Your Workflow) each appear in three or more pathways. AI-5 appears in all five. If you are choosing where to start with a small pilot group, start there.

02 · How it runs

The anatomy of a session

Most lessons run to the same 45-minute shape, so a pathway is predictable to schedule and an attendee always knows what is coming next. Two run longer, and they are noted below.

0 to 5 min
Why this matters. The stakes for this specific room, in plain language. AI-1 opens on the observation that people rarely get replaced by AI, and that they get out-run by the colleague who learned it first.
5 to 25 min
Teach. Five numbered concepts, each with an analogy and a worked example from the attendee's own job. The trainer sets the ELI level to match the room before starting.
25 to 35 min
Live demo. The trainer does the thing on screen, on the client's own material where possible, including the part where the tool gets it wrong and gets corrected. Each lesson's demo is listed in the catalog below.
35 to 42 min
Hands-on. Attendees do it themselves on their own laptops, with the trainer and one floater circulating. This is where most of the learning lands.
42 to 45 min
Recap and Monday action. A one-line summary and one named task to repeat at their desk that week.

The two exceptions. AX-8 Build Your Workflow is a build session and runs 120 minutes, because each attendee designs, builds, tests, and documents a workflow with a trainer beside them, then presents it to the room. AX-9 Dashboards, Sites and Reports runs 90 minutes, because attendees leave with a working page rather than a demonstration of one. The pathway hours quoted below already include both.

Scheduling a pathway

Counting the two longer lessons and adding breaks, each pathway lands as follows:

  • P2 Marketing Fundamentals, 5 lessons, 3 hours 45 minutes of teaching. One half day.
  • P1 AI Fundamentals, 8 lessons, 6 hours. One full day.
  • P5 AI Systems and Automation, 9 lessons including AX-8 and AX-9, 8 hours 45 minutes. One full day, or two half days.
  • P4 Owner's Fast-Track, 10 lessons including AX-8, 8 hours 45 minutes. One long day, or two half days.
  • P3 AI for Marketers, 12 lessons including AX-8 and AX-9, 11 hours. Two days.

We can run consecutive days, or split a pathway into weekly half-day sessions so the team applies each block before the next one. The weekly split gets better retention, the consecutive block is easier on rosters.

What we need in the room

  • One laptop per attendee, with the AI tool already installed and logged in. We send a setup sheet a week ahead and run a 20-minute check the morning of day one.
  • A projector or large screen, and stable Wi-Fi that does not block the AI tools. This is the single most common failure, so we test it in advance.
  • Real material to work on: your actual FAQs, past inquiries, a recent report, a live ad account. Sanitized where it needs to be. AI-8 covers how to sanitize it, and where a cohort is on a pathway without AI-8 we brief the redaction rules before the session instead.
  • Maximum 20 attendees per trainer for the hands-on segments to stay useful.

AI track  General AI capability, useful to every department

AI-1

AI Basics: what AI actually is, and isn't

★
Covers

This lesson explains what people mean by "AI" today, and the hierarchy underneath it: machine learning, generative AI, and large language models. Attendees see how an answer is produced, one predicted chunk at a time, with no lookup step and no reasoning behind it. We cover what the technology is strong at, which is drafts, rewrites, summaries, and variations at volume, and where it fails, which is facts it was not given, exact arithmetic, recent events, and anything carrying accountability. We treat hallucination as a structural consequence of predicting plausible text rather than a bug a better prompt removes. The lesson closes on what happens to data typed into a public tool.

Walk away able to

Explain in one sentence what generative AI is, name two things it does well and two it does badly, and state the rule that keeps you safe: check the facts, guard the data.

Live demo

We ask the same tool the same question twice and show the answers differ, which makes the guessing visible. Then we ask for three statistics about the client's own market, receive clean and specific numbers, and fact-check them live on screen until one of them turns out to be invented.

AI-2

AI Literacy: talking to AI so it gives you something useful

Covers

This lesson explains why the same question produces a mediocre answer for one person and a strong one for another. Attendees learn to give context up front rather than drip-feeding it, to assign a role and an audience, and to ask for a specific format. We cover how to iterate on a weak answer instead of starting over, and when to abandon a thread and open a fresh one. The lesson finishes on diagnosis, where an attendee reads a bad output and works out which part of the request caused it.

Walk away able to

Turn a one-line request into a structured brief, and fix a disappointing answer in two follow-ups instead of giving up on the tool.

Live demo

We take a vague prompt from someone in the room, run it as-is so everyone sees the generic result, then rewrite it in three visible passes: add context, add role and audience, add format and constraints. The room watches the same question produce usable output by the third pass.

AI-3

Prompting and Tools: the structure, and which tool for which job

★
Covers

This lesson gives attendees a reusable prompt structure covering role, context, task, format, and constraints, and shows why examples work better than descriptions of what you want. We go through the current tool market and what each one is better at, covering ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Microsoft Copilot, and Perplexity. Attendees then learn to save their work so they never retype it, using custom instructions, saved prompts, project spaces, and a shared team prompt library.

Walk away able to

Write a prompt that works the first time for a task you repeat, save it where a colleague can reuse it, and pick the right tool instead of defaulting to whichever one you opened first.

Live demo

We take a task the room brings, build the prompt template live against the five-part structure, then save it as a Claude Project or a Custom GPT and hand it to a second attendee to run cold. If it works in their hands without explanation, the template is good.

AI-4

AI Systems: from one-off chats to something repeatable

Covers

This lesson closes the gap between a person who prompts well and a team that has a system. Attendees build projects and knowledge bases that hold their own documents, so the tool answers from your policies rather than from the internet. We cover custom instructions at team level, and connecting AI to files, drives, and other tools. The lesson ends on judgment, where attendees decide what to automate, what to keep human-assisted, and what to leave alone.

Walk away able to

Build a shared workspace loaded with your own reference documents, so any colleague asking a policy question gets the same grounded answer.

Live demo

We load the client's own handbook, price list, or policy documents into a project workspace during the session, then ask it a question that has a precise institutional answer. We show it citing the source document, and then we ask a question the documents do not cover, so the room sees the difference between grounded and guessed.

AI-5

Agentic AI and Claude Code: AI that carries out the task

★
Covers

This lesson separates a chatbot that answers from an agent that acts, reads files, runs steps, checks its own output, and reports back. We present Claude Code as a general-purpose work agent rather than a programming tool, which is the framing most people miss. Attendees learn how permissions and approval steps work, and what an agent should never do unsupervised. The lesson names both sides honestly, covering where agents save hours and where they quietly create risk.

Walk away able to

Point an agent at a folder of your own files, ask a question that requires reading all of them, and get a sourced answer plus a formatted document, without writing any code.

Live demo

We take a real folder from the client, a stack of application PDFs, a quarter of invoices, or a set of past reports, and ask a question no one could answer without opening every file. The room watches the agent work through them and produce a sourced answer, then we have it turn that answer into a formatted report. This lesson appears in all five pathways because it is the one that changes what people think is possible.

AI-6

Generative AI: images, video, and voice

Covers

This lesson explains how image and video models differ from text models, and why that changes the way you prompt them. Attendees learn the anatomy of a visual prompt, covering subject, style, composition, lighting, and what to exclude, and they learn to hold brand consistency across a set rather than getting one lucky image. We cover voice and video generation and judge where each is currently believable. The lesson also handles licensing, usage rights, and disclosure, and it names the failure modes, starting with text inside images.

Walk away able to

Produce a consistent set of on-brand visuals from one prompt template, and recognize when a generated asset is not fit to publish.

Live demo

We generate a set of images from a single template, iterate the prompt on screen to bring them onto brand, then deliberately ask for text inside an image so the room sees it fail, and show the workaround of adding the text afterward in a design tool.

AI-7

AI on a Budget: what to pay for, and what not to

Covers

This lesson sets out what you give up on a free tier in specific terms: usage limits, weaker models, no file handling, no team sharing, and inputs used for training. Attendees compare per-seat subscriptions against API and token pricing, and find the point where each one wins. We cover matching tiers to roles rather than buying one plan for everyone, and using cheaper or open models for tasks that do not need frontier quality. The lesson ends on tool sprawl, where four departments each buy a different subscription for the same job.

Walk away able to

Build the per-seat cost model for your own headcount and defend the number to whoever signs it off.

Live demo

We run one identical, realistic task on a free tier and a paid tier side by side on screen, so the difference is observed rather than claimed. Then we build the annual cost table for the client's actual headcount live in the session, which becomes the AI economics section of this document made specific to you.

AI-8

Data Privacy with AI: what you can and cannot paste

Covers

This lesson covers what happens to text typed into each tier of each tool, and which tiers train on your inputs by default. Attendees work through Malaysia's PDPA and, for teams handling UK and European student records, the GDPR obligations that travel with that data. We go through student, applicant, employee, and customer records and decide which of them can never enter a general tool. Attendees then learn practical redaction patterns that keep the work useful, and they draft an approved-tools policy staff will follow rather than a ban they will route around.

Walk away able to

Configure your own account correctly, apply a redaction pattern to a real record, and state the rule for what never leaves the building.

Live demo

We open the actual settings screens on each major tool and turn training off, live, so nobody has to take our word for where the toggle is. Then we take a real record with personal details in it, redact it on screen, and show the AI still does the job on the redacted version.

MK-1

Marketing Foundation: audience, offer, message, channel

Covers

This lesson covers the four decisions every piece of marketing rests on, and the order to make them in. Attendees learn what a funnel is and what each stage is accountable for, and why most problems that look like a channel problem turn out to be an offer problem. The lesson gives a non-marketer the vocabulary to hold a useful conversation with an agency or an in-house team.

Walk away able to

Map your organization's own funnel on one page and name the stage where you are losing people.

Live demo

We map the client's real funnel on the board with the room, from first touch to enrolled or closed, then put the actual numbers against each stage. The leak is usually visible within ten minutes, and it is usually not where the room expected.

Marketing

Core lessons within P3: AX-2, AX-3, AX-4, AX-5, AX-6, AX-9

  • Campaign copy. Extract a voice profile from your best-performing past campaigns, then generate and edit a full set of ad variants in one sitting instead of one line at a time (AX-2).
  • Creative volume. Produce six to ten on-brand ad variants from one campus or product photograph, enough to test rather than guess (AX-3).

The cheaper route most teams miss

Per-seat subscriptions are not the only way to buy AI. Paying per unit of use through an API costs a fraction of a subscription for anything running in the background rather than in front of a person. Processing ten thousand inquiries through a small fast model costs on the order of tens of US dollars in total, not thousands.

The spread between the cheapest and the most expensive model is roughly a hundred to one, so choosing the right model for each job is the largest cost lever available. Batching work that is not time-sensitive halves the price again.
