Where you are · 03 of 04

Three people decide whether one booking turns into one visit.

Only one of them filled in your form. We have sorted them by the part each one plays in the decision. Age and postcode tell you nothing useful here.

The student who booked

They decide by picturing themselves there. They booked in a burst of motivation weeks ago, often several universities in one sitting.

  • What loses them: they can no longer picture the day, and another university's day is easier to picture
  • What wins them: their subject, their session and their time, written down for them

The person driving them

They decide by cost and effort. A parent, a carer or an older sibling gives up a Sunday and pays for the fuel or the train fares.

  • What loses them: you have never asked them anything or sent them anything
  • What wins them: their own reason to come, and their own thing to do while they are on campus

The one who went quiet

They decide by drift. They are still interested and no longer sure. They left your last two emails unopened and will leave a third unopened if it looks the same.

  • What loses them: a reminder that repeats the booking confirmation back at them
  • What wins them: a different format, plus an easy way to say "I cannot make this one, book me the next"

One detail decides this: the undergraduate open day runs on a Sunday, 9.30am to 3pm. A family has to negotiate that between them. We could not find a single piece of content on your site or in your live ads that speaks to the second person in that negotiation.

A booking is one name in a form. An attendance needs a lift, a Sunday morning and somebody else's agreement, and two of those three people have never heard from you.

What "broken" means here

The code gives your location as the words "On campus" instead of a real street address, and a partial page link instead of a full one. Google asks for a proper address, so your event cannot show up in the event results you want it in. A developer fixes this in an afternoon.

What we suggest · The five messages

Four emails and one text, sent one a week for five weeks.

We write all five. Each has one job, one format and one recipient, and each goes out on a set day counted back from the event. Email 2 is addressed to the parent, not to the student.

Email 1

On booking · 5 weeks out

Make it real right away

A calendar file with the address already inside it, their subject session named, and one line on what they will be doing that day. This replaces "we will be in touch".

Email 2

3 weeks out · To the parent

Ask the driver

A short note written to whoever is bringing them: parking, travel time, what there is for them between 10 and 3, and the two talks worth sitting in on.

Email 3

2 weeks out

Put a face on it

Ninety seconds from the academic running their subject session, saying what they will be doing in that room. One person speaks to them from inside the building.

Email 4

1 week out

Hand over the running order

Their personal schedule: arrival, session, tour, the gap in the middle and what to do with it. Your schedule page, rewritten as one person's Sunday.

Text

The day before

Clear the last excuses

Weather, trains, which door to walk in and who to look for. Plus a one-tap "I cannot make it, book me the next one" that keeps the relationship alive.

Who sends them

Your team does, out of GeckoEngage. We write the copy, build the calendar file and the running order, and specify the send triggers.

The mistake we avoid

Sending five versions of the same reminder. If two of the five could swap places without anyone noticing, we delete one of them.

Email 2 is the one nobody sends

Across the five universities we checked, we found no message written to the parent giving up their Sunday. They are the person who cancels the trip.

Our recommendation. The five-week shape is our starting assumption and it is the first thing your booking data would correct. If most bookings land ten days out, this becomes three messages and we rebuild it that way before you spend anything. The day-before text assumes you hold mobile numbers and permission to text them. If you do not, it becomes a fifth email.
